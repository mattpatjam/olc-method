## Why This Session Exists

Session 1 built what you're selling. Session 2 taught you to listen before you pitch. This session closes the loop: how to ask for the decision and make sure every conversation ends with a committed next step.

The risk without this session: discovery is working, the buyer is interested — and then it stalls at "I'll think about it" because nobody asked for the decision directly.

---

## The Big Domino — Before Any Story

Before any script or story, both salespeople need to internalize one idea: the entire pitch is organized around a single belief. Not five value points. One.

That belief is The Sanctuary's Big Domino:

> "The Sanctuary is the only wellness real estate exit in Bali that generates income while you own it, in a project that will never be replicated — with 14 units left."

Every story, every close, every follow-up points back to this. When a buyer raises an objection, you don't debate the objection. You return to the domino. If they believe that statement, every other concern becomes secondary.

**For Susi:** The domino stops her from pitching scattered features. Pick one, point back to it, stop talking.
**For Natasha:** The domino gives her a home base. When she feels uncertain, she returns to this sentence.

---

## The Epiphany Bridge — Before Any Script

Most closing training jumps straight to language patterns and objection scripts. That's the wrong starting point.

Buyers don't close because of logic. They close because of emotion — and then they use logic to justify what they've already decided. If you lead with the value stack and the ROI numbers, you're asking them to make a logical decision. Most people are not wired to do that under pressure.

The Epiphany Bridge is a short story — 60 seconds — that creates the emotional state before you present anything. It makes the buyer feel what another buyer felt at the moment of decision. When they're in that state, the scripts and the logic land differently. They're not processing information anymore. They're recognizing themselves in a story.

**The rule:** Story first. Logic after. Always.

---

## The Three Buyer Epiphany Stories

One per profile. Spoken naturally, not read. 60 seconds or less. Third-grade language — no jargon, no projections, no percentages. The goal is that the buyer feels the other person's decision moment.

---

### Pure Investor

> "I had a client last year — European, based in Singapore, had been looking at property in Southeast Asia for two years. Every time he found something, the ROI projections were aggressive and the developer was unknown. He'd pass. When we walked through The Sanctuary, I remember him stopping at the income pool model. He went quiet for a minute. Then he said: 'This is the first project where I actually understand how I make money.' He signed within the week. He wasn't buying a hotel suite. He was buying the feeling of finally finding something that made sense."

**When to use:** Buyer's main driver is financial return. They've seen other projects. They're skeptical but still looking.

---

### Lifestyle Investor

> "A woman I worked with a few months ago — she comes to Bali two or three times a year, loves Nirvana, has been a member for years. She kept saying 'it's too much money.' Then one visit she realized she was spending close to $8,000 a year on accommodation and club memberships here. We ran the numbers together. At The Sanctuary, the property covers her stays and generates income on top. She looked at me and said: 'So I'm basically paying to own the place instead of renting it every time.' That was the moment she decided."

**When to use:** Buyer has an emotional connection to Bali or Nirvana. The investment case matters but it's secondary to lifestyle.

---

### Nirvana Member

> "One of my clients had been a member here since almost the beginning. Three years ago. She'd seen the gym built, seen the restaurant open, watched the community form around her. When Tower 1 launched she said: 'I feel like I'm already home here — I just don't own any of it.' We sat down, looked at the numbers, and she bought three weeks later. She told me afterward: 'It stopped feeling like an investment and started feeling like belonging somewhere permanently.'"

**When to use:** Buyer is already a Nirvana member or regular visitor. They know the property, they love the experience, but haven't made the leap to owning.

## Combined Opening Exercise — Building the Story Inventory (20 min, both in the room)

The three stories above cover the first false belief category: "Is this real? Does this opportunity actually work?" That's the new opportunity story.

Two more categories come up constantly at The Sanctuary and neither has a story yet:

**Internal belief:** "A $200K investment isn't the kind of decision someone like me makes." The buyer believes in the project — they don't believe in themselves as the type of person who makes this kind of move.

**External belief:** "The lease extension is uncertain / the market is slowing / my financial advisor will say no." The buyer is ready internally but convinced some outside force will kill the deal.

### How the exercise runs

Ask the team — together, before splitting into individual sessions:

**Step 1 — Surface the belief (5 min)**
"What's the most common thing a buyer says right before they go quiet? Not the objection they give you — the real hesitation underneath it."

Let both answer. The answers will cluster around internal and external categories. Write them on a whiteboard or paper: these are the false beliefs.

**Step 2 — Find the experience (5 min)**
"What do you think happened to this person before they came to us? What made them believe this?"

Most buyers have a past experience that created the belief — a bad investment, a cautious spouse, a financial advisor who always says no. The team usually knows this from discovery conversations. Surface it.

**Step 3 — Build the story (10 min)**
"Does either of you have a real client who had this exact fear and still closed? What happened?"

This is the raw material. Matthieu shapes it into a 60-second story using the same format as the three stories above: set the scene, one moment of recognition, what shifted, what they said at the end.

**Target output: 2 new stories**

One for internal belief (the buyer who didn't see themselves as this type of investor), one for external belief (the buyer who had the lease/market/advisor fear and moved anyway).

These stories go directly into the individual sessions that follow. Both salespeople use them from this session onward.

## Where the Story Goes in the Flow

```
Discovery (Session 2 tools) → understand profile + surface internal/external fears
        ↓
Big Domino → anchor the conversation ("the only wellness exit in Bali with 14 units left")
        ↓
Profile Story → match buyer to one of the 3 profile stories (new opportunity belief)
        ↓
Belief Story → if hesitation surfaces, match to internal or external story from inventory
        ↓
Value Stack → outcomes, offer elements, guarantee (now they're emotionally ready)
        ↓
Closing Language → ask for the decision
        ↓
Committed Next Step → lock in date, time, and reason
```

The stories are a bridge between discovery and pitch. Match the story to the belief you're hearing, not the profile alone. Do not skip. Do not summarize. Tell it.

## Live False Belief Diagnosis — The Core Closing Skill

Mini-closes and scripted objection responses are for group presentations, where you can't get live feedback. In a 1:1 conversation, the buyer tells you exactly which false belief is blocking them — if you're listening for it.

The skill is not having better scripts. It's pattern recognition: hear the hesitation → identify the belief category → deploy the matching story.

**The four categories and what they sound like at The Sanctuary:**

| Belief category | What the buyer says | Underlying belief | Story to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| New opportunity | "I've looked at projects like this before and they never delivered" | "This category doesn't work" | Pure Investor / Lifestyle Investor / Nirvana Member (whichever profile matches) |
| Vehicle | "I don't really understand how the income model works in practice" | "I don't understand the mechanism" | Walk them through the income pool model — plain language, one real example |
| Internal | "I don't know if I'm ready for this level of investment" | "I'm not the kind of person who does this" | Internal belief story from the opening exercise |
| External | "The lease extension worries me" / "I need to check with my advisor" / "The market feels uncertain" | "Something outside me will stop this" | External belief story from the opening exercise |

**The diagnostic move:**

When a buyer hesitates, ask one question and stop:
> "What specifically is making you hesitate? I want to make sure I'm actually helping you here."

Then wait. Their answer tells you the category. Match the category to a story. Tell the story. Then return to the Big Domino.

**What not to do:** Don't argue the objection with data. Presenting more ROI numbers at someone who said "the lease worries me" doesn't resolve the belief — it adds noise. The story does what the data can't.

**For Susi:** She will instinctively respond to objections with facts. The reframe: "The data doesn't close them. It just gives them more to think about. The story closes them." One story, well-told, beats ten slides.

**For Natasha:** She will hear an objection and feel stuck because she doesn't want to push. The reframe: telling a story isn't pushing. It's sharing what happened to someone else. She's not asking them to decide — she's just describing what another buyer experienced. The decision follows naturally.

## Closing Language

### The Committed Next Step Formula

Every conversation ends with a date, a time, and a reason. "I'll send you the details" is not a close. It is a handoff to silence.

Accepted next steps:
- "Let's speak Thursday at 3pm to go through any questions you have." (get the date confirmed)
- "I'll send the numbers now — can we jump on a quick call this week?"
- "If you're ready to move forward, the next step is the reservation form and a 10% deposit to hold the unit."

Never leave without one of these locked in.

---

### Handling "I Need to Think About It"

Do not accept it. Accepting it ends the conversation without a path forward.

**Step 1 — Name it and create space:**
> "Totally fair. When you get home, what specifically are you going to think through? Walk me through it."

Then stop talking. Most buyers have no real answer. When they can't articulate it, the vagueness becomes visible to them.

**Step 2 — Surface the decision criteria out loud:**
Ask one at a time, pause after each:
- "Do you believe The Sanctuary is a real opportunity?" (usually yes)
- "Do you trust the project and the team?" (usually yes)
- "Do you think this could work for you financially?" (here's where the real hesitation surfaces)
- "Do you have access to the entry amount to get started?"

When they've answered yes to three and stalled on one — that's the real conversation. Address the one thing, not the vague whole.

**Step 3 — Directional decision frame:**
> "Can I ask you something? Both options here are actually risk-free. You walk out and nothing changes — that's one path. Or you take this step, knowing it gets you closer to what you told me you want: [income / freedom / something you own in Bali / being part of this community]. One option has potential upside. The other stays the same. Which one are you more comfortable with?"

This reframe removes the pressure of certainty. They don't have to believe it's the perfect decision — they just have to believe it moves them forward.

**Note for Natasha:** Lead with the directional frame. It doesn't feel like pressure — it's just clarifying direction. It's the close she can use without feeling like she's forcing anything.

---

### Handling "I Need to Talk to My Lawyer / Financial Advisor / Partner"

What the buyer is saying underneath: "I don't feel empowered to make this decision myself." The advisor is rarely the real objection — the buyer is. The named person (lawyer, advisor, spouse) isn't in the room, so they'll answer as if speaking for them — but they're revealing their own concern.

**Three-step process:**

**Step 1 — Isolate the real objection:**
> "Of course — I fully understand. Out of curiosity, what part of this do you think they might have concerns about?"

Let them answer. Whatever they name (lease extension, liquidity, timing, return certainty) — that's the real conversation. Handle it now with a story or the relevant proof. The advisor is not the blocker. That belief is.

**Step 2 — Address it directly:**
Once the real concern is named, use the Live False Belief Diagnosis tools from above. If it's a lease concern — external story. If it's "not sure I can make this kind of move" — internal story. Don't debate the concern; break the belief behind it.

**Step 3 — Permission vs. support reframe:**
> "Can I ask you something? Are you looking for their permission to do this — or for their support after you've already decided?"

Those are two different conversations. Permission keeps them stuck (if the advisor says no, they stop). Support means they've made the call and they're bringing their advisor in to help execute it.

Follow: "Most people who've made a decision like this came in looking for permission and left realizing they were looking for support. Because ultimately you're the one who has to live with the outcome — and you're the one who did the research, made the trip, and sat in this conversation."

Then return to the Big Domino. Does their concern change whether The Sanctuary is the only wellness exit in Bali with 14 units left? Usually not.

**Note for Susi:** Her instinct is to defend the project when a third party is invoked. Don't. The advisor isn't the opponent. The belief is. Isolate, address the belief, return to the domino.

---

### Handling "It's Too Expensive / I Don't Have That Kind of Capital Right Now"

Never accept this at face value. It is almost always a value perception problem, not a resource problem. Test this internally: if you offered the unit for free, would they find the money? Yes. So the issue is perceived value, not affordability.

**Response 1 — Shift the reference point:**
> "Let me ask you — how much do you typically spend when you come to Bali? Accommodation, Nirvana membership, restaurants, activities?"

Run the number with them: $6–8K per visit × 2–3 visits per year = $12–24K a year on something they don't own that generates nothing. Over 25 years, that's potentially $300K spent on renting what they could own.

> "At The Sanctuary, that spend becomes ownership — and the property generates income on top of that. You're not spending more. You're redirecting where the money goes."

**Response 2 — Resourcefulness vs. resources:**
> "I hear you on the capital. What I've seen — and this is true across every client I've worked with — is that when something genuinely mattered to them, they found a way. Not because the money appeared, but because they got resourceful. If you really wanted to make this happen, what's the first thing you'd look at?"

Let them answer. The buyer who discovers their own path owns the decision. Don't suggest solutions for them.

**Response 3 — Cost of inaction:**
> "Let me ask the question differently. What has waiting cost you over the last few years? How many projects have you looked at in Bali that are now sold out? What would you say to yourself if in 18 months these 14 units are gone and you're still renting when you're here?"

The question isn't whether they can afford the entry. The question is what the cost of not deciding continues to be.

**Note for Susi:** Her default is to show more ROI numbers. More data doesn't fix a value perception problem — a reframe does. The reference point shift (renting vs. owning) is faster and more effective than another yield table.

---

### Urgency — Honest, Not Pressured

The facts are strong. Use them plainly.

> "I want to be upfront — there are 14 units left and we're targeting August. Once those are gone, the development is closed permanently. If the timing works for you, now is genuinely the moment."

That's it. State it once. Don't repeat it. Repetition turns a fact into pressure.

## Session Coaching — By Profile

### Susi's Session (~50 min)

**Start with:** The Big Domino. Ask her to say it back in her own words without looking at it. Does she believe it? Can she say it naturally? If it sounds scripted, work on it until it doesn't.

**Teach:**
- The Epiphany Bridge — story before logic. She already saw the opening exercise, so she knows the structure. Her job now is to own the three profile stories in her own voice. Let her rewrite them — she'll do it better once she understands the structure. Frame it as a speed hack: "Buyers who hear a story first close faster. Less time on objections."
- The internal + external belief stories from the opening exercise — when to deploy them (when the hesitation is about self-doubt or external forces, not about the project itself).
- The committed next step: every interaction ends with a date, time, reason. "I'll send details" is not a next step.
- Handling objections without steamrolling: label it, wait, let them explain. "It sounds like the extension question is what's holding you back — is that right?" Then stop talking.

**Role play:** Two scenarios from her active leads. Full close, start to finish: Big Domino anchor → profile story → belief story if needed → value stack → ask for the decision → handle the objection → committed next step.

**Assign before Session 4:** One committed close attempt in every active conversation. Report back on which story landed best and which objection came up most — bring the exact words the buyer used.

**Note on Susi:** She will want to skip the story and go straight to numbers. The reframe is efficiency, not empathy: "Every time you lead with numbers and they stall, you've just added a week to the close. The story removes the stall."

### Natasha's Session (~50 min)

**Start with:** Review of her first solo intro call (assigned in Session 2). What worked, what froze her. Then anchor the Big Domino — same exercise as Susi. Can she say it naturally?

**Teach:**
- Reframe closing as helping: "My job is to help you get to a decision, not to convince you. Asking directly is doing my job."
- The Epiphany Bridge: give her all five stories (3 profile + 2 from the opening exercise) as written scripts she can read until they feel natural. She will not improvise at first — that's fine. The script is the safety net. She should also understand when to deploy each: profile stories first, belief stories when you hear internal or external hesitation surface.
- The next-step formula: "I'll send you the numbers now — can we speak Thursday to go through any questions?" Simple, direct, gives them a clear action.
- Handling "I need to think about it": "Totally fair — what specifically would you want to think through? Let's address it now."

**Role play:** Repeat the full close — Big Domino anchor → profile story → belief story if needed → value → ask → objection → next step — until asking for the decision doesn't freeze her. As many reps as it takes. This is the only metric for this session.

**Assign before Session 4:** Attempt a committed close in every active conversation. Use whichever story matches what you're hearing. Write down the exact words the buyer used when they hesitated — bring it to Session 4.

**Note on Natasha:** She will embrace the stories immediately — she's an SC, naturally empathetic and narrative-driven. The breakthrough is that asking for the decision doesn't feel like pressure when it follows a story. The story creates the emotional readiness; the ask is just the logical next step. The internal belief story is especially powerful for her to deliver — she genuinely means it, and buyers will feel that.
