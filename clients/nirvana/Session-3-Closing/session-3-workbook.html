The Big Domino
·
Before Any Script, One Belief
5 minutes · both in the room

What is a Big Domino? (aka: the one belief, the anchor statement) Knock over the first domino in a row and every other domino falls on its own — you never touch them individually. A sales conversation works the same way: instead of chasing every price question, timing worry, or comparison one at a time, you get the buyer to believe one foundational statement. Once they believe it, the smaller objections stop being reasons to walk away — they become details to work through.

That's why the entire pitch is organized around a single belief, not five value points. When a buyer raises an objection, you don't debate the objection — you return to the domino. If they believe this statement, every other concern becomes secondary.

"The Sanctuary is the only wellness real estate exit in Bali that generates income while you own it, in a project that will never be replicated — with 14 units left."
For Susi

The domino stops scattered feature-pitching. Pick one, point back to it, stop talking.

For Natasha

The domino gives you a home base. When you feel uncertain, return to this sentence.

Say it back in your own words. Does it sound natural, not scripted?

The Epiphany Bridge
1
Story First, Logic After
Why closing starts with a story, not a script

Buyers don't close because of logic. They close because of emotion, then use logic to justify what they've already decided. Lead with the value stack and the ROI numbers, and you're asking them to make a logical decision under pressure. Most people aren't wired to do that.

What is an Epiphany Bridge? (aka: the story you tell before the numbers) It's a short story, 60 seconds, that creates the emotional state before you present anything. It makes the buyer feel what another buyer felt at the moment of decision. When they're in that state, the scripts and the logic land differently — they're recognizing themselves in a story, not processing information.

The rule: Story first. Logic after. Always.

The Four Reasons Buyers Hesitate (aka: False Belief Categories)

Every hesitation a buyer has traces back to one of these four reasons. Match the reason to the right story instead of arguing the objection with facts — a story dissolves it, data just adds more to think about (more on why in Reading It Live, page 6).

A
"This kind of deal doesn't work" (category name: New opportunity) — "I've looked at projects like this before and they never delivered." Story: match the buyer's profile (Pure Investor, Lifestyle Investor, Nirvana Member).
Example: the Singapore investor who'd been burned by aggressive projections elsewhere — until the income pool model finally made sense to him. Full story: Story Inventory Part 1, page 4.
B
"I don't get how the money part works" (category name: Vehicle) — "I don't understand how the income model works." Walk them through the income pool model in plain language, one real example.
Example: "Here's what a comparable unit actually paid out last quarter" — one real number beats a projection sheet.
C
Self-doubt (category name: Internal) — "I don't know if I'm ready for this level of investment." They believe: "I'm not the kind of person who does this."
Example shape: a buyer who never saw himself as "the investor type" until he realized he'd already run three small businesses without calling it that. Your own version: Story Inventory Part 2, built live on page 5.
D
Fear something outside will block it (category name: External) — "The lease extension worries me" / "I need to check with my advisor." They believe: "Something outside me will stop this."
Example shape: a buyer stalled for months on a lease clause elsewhere, then realized it was standard and had never stopped another buyer. Your own version: Story Inventory Part 2, built live on page 5.

Story Inventory — Part 1
2
The Three Profile Stories
Spoken naturally, 60 seconds or less — no jargon, no percentages

How it all connects, in order: belief surfaces in Discovery → anchor with the Big Domino (page 2) → tell the matching Epiphany Bridge story — that's Story Inventory Part 1, the 3 profile stories below → if a deeper hesitation surfaces, tell the matching belief story — Story Inventory Part 2, Internal or External, built on page 5 → now the value stack → the ask → committed next step.

Pure Investor Financial driver, seen other projects, still skeptical
When: right after the Big Domino, before the value stack — buyer's driver is financial return and they've seen other projects.
"I had a client last year, European, based in Singapore, looking at Southeast Asia property for two years. Every project he found had aggressive ROI projections and an unknown developer. When we walked through The Sanctuary, he stopped at the income pool model. Went quiet a minute. Then said: 'This is the first project where I actually understand how I make money.' Signed within the week. He wasn't buying a hotel suite. He was buying the feeling of finally finding something that made sense."
Lifestyle Investor Emotional connection to Bali/Nirvana, investment case is secondary
When: right after the Big Domino, before the value stack — buyer has an emotional pull to Bali/Nirvana, the investment case is secondary.
"A woman I worked with a few months ago comes to Bali two or three times a year, loves Nirvana, been a member for years. Kept saying 'it's too much money.' Then one visit she realized she was spending close to $8,000 a year on accommodation and memberships here. We ran the numbers together. At The Sanctuary that spend becomes ownership, and the property generates income on top. She said: 'So I'm basically paying to own the place instead of renting it every time.' That was the moment she decided."
Nirvana Member Already a member or regular visitor, hasn't made the leap to owning
When: right after the Big Domino, before the value stack — buyer already knows and loves the property but hasn't made the leap to owning.
"One of my clients had been a member here since almost the beginning, three years. She'd seen the gym built, the restaurant open, the community form around her. When Tower 1 launched she said: 'I feel like I'm already home here, I just don't own any of it.' We sat down, looked at the numbers, she bought three weeks later. Told me afterward: 'It stopped feeling like an investment and started feeling like belonging somewhere permanently.'"

Build the Internal & External Belief Stories
20 minutes — both in the room, live exercise

The exercise (aka: building Story Inventory Part 2): together, build two new stories — one that breaks the Internal false belief, one that breaks the External false belief (see page 3). Work through the three questions below in order, out loud. By the end, each of you writes the finished story in the boxes at the bottom, in plain spoken language, 60 seconds or less. These become the belief stories you deploy in Live False Belief Diagnosis (page 6) any time that hesitation surfaces.

1
Surface the belief — 5 min
"What's the most common thing a buyer says right before they go quiet? Not the objection they give you, the real hesitation underneath it."
Let both answer. Write the answers down — they'll cluster into internal (self-doubt) and external (outside forces).
2
Find the experience — 5 min
"What do you think happened to this person before they came to us? What made them believe this?"
A past bad investment, a cautious spouse, an advisor who always says no. Surface it from real discovery conversations.
3
Build the story — 10 min
"Does either of you have a real client who had this exact fear and still closed? What happened?"
This is the raw material. Shape it live: set the scene, one moment of recognition, what shifted, what they said at the end.

The Core Closing Skill
4
Reading It Live (aka: Live False Belief Diagnosis)
Pattern recognition, not better scripts

In a 1:1 conversation, the buyer tells you exactly which of the four reasons is blocking them, if you're listening for it. Hear the hesitation, identify which of the four it is, deploy the matching story.

What they say Underlying belief Story to use
"I've looked at projects like this before" This category doesn't work Profile story (Pure Investor / Lifestyle / Member)
"I don't understand the income model" I don't understand the mechanism Walk through the income pool model, plain language
"I don't know if I'm ready for this" I'm not the kind of person who does this Internal belief story
"The lease extension worries me" / "check with advisor" Something outside me will stop this External belief story
The diagnostic move — ask, then stop talking:
"What specifically is making you hesitate? I want to make sure I'm actually helping you here."
Alternate diagnostic — the confidence-gap question (aka the "what's missing" close):
"Putting the Big Domino aside for a second — what would need to be true today for you to feel 100% confident saying yes? What's missing?"

Whatever they name is the last false belief standing between them and the reservation. Match it to a category above, deploy the story, then return to the Big Domino.

What not to do:

· Don't argue the objection with data — more ROI numbers at someone who said "the lease worries me" adds noise, not resolution. The story does what data can't.

· Don't say "But look at the returns again" or "the numbers speak for themselves" — that's data, not belief work.

· Don't say "I understand, but…" — it signals you're about to argue, not that you actually heard them.

· Don't fill the silence after the diagnostic question. Let them answer, even if it takes ten seconds.

· Don't repeat the urgency line ("14 units left") as a rebuttal — it turns a fact into pressure.

Closing Language
5
The Committed Next Step Formula
Every conversation ends with a date, a time, and a reason

"I'll send you the details" is not a close. It is a handoff to silence. Never leave a conversation without one of these locked in:

01
Confirm a date "Let's speak Thursday at 3pm to go through any questions you have."
02
Book the follow-up "I'll send the numbers now, can we jump on a quick call this week?"
03
Move to paperwork "If you're ready to move forward, the next step is the reservation form and a 10% deposit to hold the unit."
Urgency — honest, not pressured. State once, don't repeat:
"I want to be upfront, there are 14 units left and we're targeting August. Once those are gone, the development is closed permanently. If the timing works for you, now is genuinely the moment."

Objection Script 1 of 3
✓
"I Need to Think About It"
Do not accept it. Accepting it ends the conversation without a path forward.
1
Name it and create space, then stop talking:
"Totally fair. When you get home, what specifically are you going to think through? Walk me through it." Most buyers have no real answer — the vagueness becomes visible to them.
2
Surface the decision criteria out loud, one at a time:
"Do you believe The Sanctuary is a real opportunity?" (usually yes) · "Do you trust the project and team?" (usually yes) · "Do you think this could work for you financially?" (real hesitation surfaces here) · "Do you have access to the entry amount?"
3
Or go straight to the confidence-gap question (aka the "what's missing" close — see page 6):
"Putting the Big Domino aside for a second — what would need to be true today for you to feel 100% confident saying yes? What's missing?" Whatever they name is the real objection. Address it with the matching false-belief story, then move to the frame below.
4
Directional decision frame:
"Both options here are actually risk-free. You walk out and nothing changes — that's one path. Or you take this step, knowing it gets you closer to what you told me you want. One option has potential upside. The other stays the same. Which one are you more comfortable with?"

Note for Natasha: lead with the directional frame. It doesn't feel like pressure, it's clarifying direction. This is the close you can use without feeling like you're forcing anything.

Objection Script 2 of 3
✓
"I Need to Talk to My Lawyer / Advisor / Partner"
The named person isn't the real objection — the buyer is.
1
Isolate the real objection:
"Of course, I fully understand. Out of curiosity, what part of this do you think they might have concerns about?" Whatever they name is the real conversation.
2
Address it directly:
Use the false-belief diagnosis: lease concern → external story. "Not sure I can make this move" → internal story. Don't debate the concern, break the belief behind it.
3
Permission vs. support reframe:
"Are you looking for their permission to do this, or their support after you've already decided?" Follow: "Most people who've made a decision like this came in looking for permission and left realizing they were looking for support." Then return to the Big Domino.

Note for Susi: your instinct is to defend the project when a third party is invoked. Don't. The advisor isn't the opponent. Isolate, address the belief, return to the domino.

Objection Script 3 of 3
✓
"It's Too Expensive / I Don't Have That Capital Right Now"
Almost always a value perception problem, not a resource problem.
1
Shift the reference point:
"How much do you typically spend when you come to Bali? Accommodation, membership, restaurants?" Run it: $6–8K/visit × 2–3 visits/year = $12–24K/year on something they don't own. "At The Sanctuary, that spend becomes ownership, and the property generates income on top. You're not spending more, you're redirecting where the money goes."
2
Resourcefulness vs. resources:
"When something genuinely mattered to a client, they found a way. Not because the money appeared, but because they got resourceful. If you really wanted to make this happen, what's the first thing you'd look at?" Let them answer — don't suggest solutions for them.
3
Cost of inaction:
"What has waiting cost you over the last few years? How many projects have you looked at that are now sold out? What would you say to yourself if in 18 months these 14 units are gone and you're still renting when you're here?"

Note for Susi: your default is to show more ROI numbers. More data doesn't fix a value perception problem, a reframe does. The reference point shift is faster and more effective than another yield table.
