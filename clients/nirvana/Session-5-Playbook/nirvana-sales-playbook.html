Sales Process from Contact to Close
Six stages. Everything after this page is the depth behind each one.

Read this page first, every time you're unsure what to do next on a live deal. Each stage below has one goal and one core move. The section number tells you where to go for the full script.

Two rules apply at every stage, not just one of them. Every call opens with a quick frame, why you're calling, how it'll run, so it feels like a process, not an improvised pitch. What You're Selling, and How Much of It to Say
One strong proof point beats a list of good ones

The one-sentence offer: "Guaranteed minimum 10% ROI without the nightmare of daily management, even if you've never invested in Bali before." Below is what backs it up. Lead with your single strongest row, the guarantee, and keep the rest in reserve for when they ask. Piling on proof points weakens a pitch more than it strengthens one, a buyer's impression is built from the average of what you tell them, not the total.

Tower 1: Same Offer, Different Buyer
Never a discount version of Tower 2

Tower 1 ground floor keeps getting framed as the lesser choice. No buyer consciously wants the second-best option at nearly the same price. Fix: these are two distinct offers for two distinct buyers, not one offer with a discount attached.

Pure Investor Pitch

"You're not paying $80K extra for a sauna you'd use 28 days a year. You're investing $179K for the exact same ROI percentage as a $259K unit, same income pool, same guarantee, same management. That $80K keeps working for you elsewhere."

Club Floor Pitch

"Ground level means you wake up inside the facility. No elevator, direct terrace access, 30 steps from the gym, the pool, the restaurant. Tower 2 has the view. Tower 1 has the life."

Tower 1 Extras

3-year ROI guarantee (vs. 2 years on Tower 2, pending Toby's sign-off) · priority booking on the 28 personal-use days · Founding Investor recognition, last units in the development ever (pending sign-off) · monthly construction update.

What to Avoid
  • Never compare Tower 1 and Tower 2 in the same sentence. "Same but without the sauna" kills it immediately.
  • Don't lead with square meters or floor level. Lead with the buyer type.
  • Don't discount the price. It devalues the brand and insults the 18 buyers who already paid full price.
"Based on what you've told me, you want a passive investment, no management headache, strong return, Tower 1 is actually the better fit. Same guarantee, same management, same brand, but $179K instead of $259K. That's $80K you keep working for you elsewhere. And these are the last units in the development. When are you looking to make a decision?"

2 · Client Profiles
2
Who You're Actually Talking To
Three profiles. A guess until discovery confirms it.

Not "a boutique hotel suite in Bali." Every buyer wants something underneath that. A profile below is a hypothesis, not a label, it only becomes real once the buyer's own words in discovery (Section 3) confirm it.

Pure Investor

Wants financial security, money working without them, zero-headache passive income. Decision is driven by the math, not amenities. Real example: a Singapore-based investor who'd spent two years walking away from unverified projects stopped at the income-pool model and said "this is the first project where I actually understand how I make money." Signed within the week.

Club Floor (Lifestyle Investor)

Wants a life split between home and Bali that pays for itself, embedded in the community, not just financially exposed. Decision is driven by identity, not math. Real example: a repeat visitor already spending roughly $8,000/year on accommodation and membership realized "I'm basically paying to own the place instead of renting it every time" once the numbers were run together.

Member Lead (Nirvana Member)

Wants to stop being a visitor and become an owner of something they already love. Highest-opportunity, most underused profile, roughly 700 people a day through the club, almost none approached. Real example: a 3-year member said "I feel like I'm already home here, I just don't own any of it" when Tower 1 launched. Bought three weeks later.

3 · Discovery
3
Open the Call, Then Discover
Every call starts with a frame and ends with a next step (Section 5)
Open With a Frame

Before any question, say why you're calling and how the call will run: "I wanted to reach out because [reason]. This'll take about ten minutes, I'll ask a few questions to understand where you're at, then share what's relevant to you." A short frame like this signals a process, not an improvised pitch, the same reason every call should end with a specific next step confirmed, never left open.

Technique

The largest sales-behavior study ever run found something counterintuitive: pitching advantages before a buyer has said what they need doesn't just fail, it creates objections, because they've got nothing yet to weigh the value against. Skipping discovery to "save time" costs more time than it saves.

You are not sellers, you are guides. The buyer is the hero, trying to reach something real, financial security, a Bali base, proof to themselves that they make bold moves. Every buyer has a stated reason and a real one, discovery is how you find the real one.

The Four Base Questions
QuestionWhat it tells you
"Are you looking at something for this year, or is this more of a 2027 situation for you?" Urgency and decision readiness. "This year" means close. "2027" means qualify differently or move on.
"Are you thinking of this primarily as an investment, or do you actually see yourself using it as a base in Bali?" Which profile you're talking to. Pure Investor vs. Club Floor. The pitch after this is completely different.
"Are you looking at other options right now, or is The Sanctuary your main focus?" How to position and how much urgency to use. Comparing means differentiate. Main focus means remove the last obstacle.
"Beyond price and timing, is there anything else that would hold you back if everything checked out?" Surfaces a real objection early, before you've pitched anything, so it doesn't blindside you at the close (Section 4).

Selective investor posture (hesitant leads and Member Leads): let part of the discovery feel like an interview, not a pitch. "What's your investing background?" or "we're selective about who we bring in at this stage" reframes the dynamic, the buyer is being considered, not deciding whether to buy. Confidence, not arrogance.

Going One Level Deeper
For a buyer who's interested but hasn't moved yet

The base questions tell you who you're talking to. They don't always tell you why someone who seems genuinely interested still hasn't acted. When that's the case, two more questions do real work, asked after the base four, not instead of them:

Surface what's held them back

"What's stopped you from making a move like this before now?"

Make waiting feel costly, honestly

"What would waiting another year actually cost you?"

These aren't tricks, they're genuine questions that most buyers haven't asked themselves out loud. Getting a real answer, in their own words, does more to move a stalled deal forward than any amount of you restating the guarantee. If they don't have a real answer, that's useful information too, it usually means the hesitation is somewhere else (see Section 4).

How Discovery Starts, in Writing
Reactivation (warm lead who went quiet)

"Hi [name], I know it's been a while. I'm reaching out because there are 14 units left and a price increase coming. Before anything moves, I want to make sure you had the full picture. Are you still thinking about this for this year?"

New lead (first message after ad opt-in)

"Hi [name], thanks for reaching out about The Sanctuary. Quick question before I send you anything, are you thinking of this as an investment, or are you actually planning to come to Bali and use it as a base?"

Member approach (on-site or warm outreach)

"Where are you staying while you're here?" [They name somewhere else.] "Did you know you can own a suite inside the club? Fully managed, lifetime membership from day one, pays you 10% a year while you're away. Worth a quick conversation?"

4 · Objections
4
Mirror It, Then Diagnose the Layer
Circumstances, then other people, then themselves
Technique

Objections stack in a predictable order: first circumstances (time, money), then other people (a partner, an advisor), then the buyer's own fear of getting it wrong. Whatever layer it's in, mirror it back first, repeat the key phrase with a questioning tone ("Too expensive?") to confirm you heard the real concern and invite them to expand, rather than answer your own assumption of what they meant. Once they've confirmed and expanded, ask a question that brings it back to size. That's the pattern for every layer below.

Layer 1 · Circumstances — "It's Too Expensive"

Mirror it first ("Too expensive?"), then: "How much do you typically spend when you come to Bali, accommodation, membership, restaurants?" Run the number: $6,000–$8,000 per visit, two to three visits a year, potentially $300,000 spent renting over 25 years. "That spend becomes ownership, and it generates income on top."

Almost always a value problem, not a resource problem. Offered for free, they'd find the money, so the real question is what makes waiting feel safer than moving.

Layer 2 · Other People — "I Need to Talk to My Partner or Advisor"

Mirror it ("Talk to your partner?"), then: "Of course, I fully understand. What part of this do you think they might have concerns about?" Whatever they name is the real conversation, the advisor isn't the opponent, the belief is.

Technique

Treat it as a position, not the real answer, then ask what interest it's protecting, usually the buyer is revealing their own concern, not the absent person's. Follow with: "are you looking for their permission, or their support after you've already decided?" Permission keeps them stuck, support means they've already decided.

Layer 3 · Self — "I Need to Think About It"

Mirror it ("Think about it?"), then: "Totally fair. When you get home, what specifically are you going to think through?" Then stop talking. Most have no real answer, and the vagueness becomes visible to them.

5 · How to Close
5
Propose the Next Step Plainly, Once
Pushing twice loses more deals than it wins
Technique

On a purchase this size, pressure-based closing measurably backfires, buyers who feel pushed report lower satisfaction even when they say yes, and one hard close performs worse than a single, plainly-stated next step. A buyer who's been genuinely heard through discovery and objections doesn't need to be closed, they close themselves. Your job at this stage is three moves, in order, not a script to push through.

1
Check nothing's left unsaid. Ask directly if any concern is still sitting there before you propose anything.
2
Summarize what they actually told you, in their words, tied to what they said they needed. Then confirm it lands: you'll know it landed when you hear "that's right," not just "you're right." The second one means they're placating you, not agreeing.
3
Propose one specific next step, stated plainly, not asked tentatively. "The next step is X" beats "would you maybe want to X?"
The One Line That Anchors Everything
"The Sanctuary is the only wellness real estate exit in Bali that generates income while you own it, in a project that will never be replicated, with 14 units left."

Say it in your own words, not read off a page. When an objection comes up, don't debate it, return to this. If they believe this sentence, every other concern becomes secondary.

5 · How to Close
·
Committed Next Step, Every Time
"I'll send you the details" is a handoff to silence, not a next step

Every conversation ends with a date, a time, and a reason. Accepted versions:

  • "Let's speak Thursday at 3pm to go through any questions you have."
  • "I'll send the numbers now, can we jump on a quick call this week?"
  • "If you're ready to move forward, the next step is the reservation form and a 10% deposit."
If You're Not Sure the Yes Is Real

Two questions test whether agreement is genuine: "How will we know we're on track?" and "How will we handle it if we're off track?" A vague or hesitant answer, or a reply of "I'll try," means the yes isn't solid yet, keep asking until they state it in their own words.

No Reason Left frame, for a genuinely stuck conversation: "Both options here have no real downside. Walking away changes nothing today. Moving forward has real upside. If there's no risk either way, which option actually gives you something?" Best case, their life changes. Worst case, they got closer to a decision they were already circling. Neither is a bad outcome for them.

6 · Follow-Up
6
Earn the Reply, Don't Just Ask For It
Never "just checking in." Always bring something.
The Structure: Give → Tease → Ask
Give

A real update or fact. Construction progress, units sold, a price change.

Tease

Let urgency show through what you gave. What they'd miss by waiting.

Ask

One clear, specific, low-friction question.

Magic Urgency Line

"Have you given up on making this happen for you?"

Deliberately client-centered. Their goal was never Nirvana's success, it's their own. A quiet lead is far more likely to reply and deny giving up than to stay silent.

What To Do When It's Not In Here
A situation will come up that isn't covered. Here's the judgment.

Three steps, in order.

1
Use your own judgment first.
Most situations are a variation on something already in this playbook, not something genuinely new.
2
Fall back to the guide, hero frame.
You're the guide, the buyer is the hero. The question is always "what does the hero need to take the next step," not "what's the exact right line." When in doubt, ask a discovery question rather than guess. That frame covers situations no script can.
3
Flag real gaps instead of improvising.
Pricing, terms, anything that needs Toby's sign-off, or a genuinely new objection isn't yours to decide alone. Flag it to Toby or Matthieu.

This playbook is built to work without anyone standing over your shoulder, whether you're on the team today or joining later. The process on page 2 is the whole shape of the job. Everything after it is depth for the moment you need it.
